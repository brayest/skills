# ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — AI Management System (AIMS)

**What it is:** the first international, **certifiable** standard for managing AI. It does for AI what
ISO 27001 did for information security — it specifies a *management system*, not a set of technical
controls, and an accredited body can audit us against it and issue a certificate.

**What it is not:** a technical standard. It says nothing about model architecture, and it will not
tell you how to stop prompt injection. It tells you how to *run an organization* that builds AI
responsibly, and how to prove you do.

> **Copyright note.** ISO/IEC 42001 is a paywalled standard. This file describes its structure and
> requirements in our own words, triangulated from certification-body sources (BSI, Schellman,
> A-LIGN, Coalfire, DNV). It does not reproduce the standard's text. **Buy the standard** (~CHF 180

## 1. Why it exists in a PDCA shape

42001 uses the ISO Harmonized Structure (same skeleton as 27001, 9001), so it runs on
**Plan-Do-Check-Act**: plan (policy, scope, risk criteria) → do (build and deploy with controls) →
check (monitor, audit, review) → act (correct, improve). The whole point is that AI risk management
is *continuous*, and the certificate attests to a loop that is actually turning — not to a document
set that was written once.

Auditors look for exactly that linkage: **policy → objectives → risk assessment → Statement of
Applicability → implemented controls → monitoring → internal audit → management review → corrective
action.** A binder of policies with no evidence the loop ran is the classic failure.

## 2. Clauses 4–10 — the mandatory requirements

These are the "shall" clauses. Everything here is required; Annex A controls (§3) are selectable.

**Clause 4 — Context of the organization**
- 4.1 Identify internal/external issues affecting the AIMS.
- 4.2 Identify interested parties and their requirements (for a platform vendor: the client, the
  client's end users, regulators, the vendor's own organization).
- 4.3 **Define and document the AIMS scope.** For us this is the decisive one — see §5 on roles.
- 4.4 Establish, implement, maintain and continually improve the AIMS.

**Clause 5 — Leadership**
- 5.1 Top management demonstrates commitment (not delegable to the engineer).
- 5.2 **A documented AI policy**, communicated.
- 5.3 Roles, responsibilities, and authorities assigned.

**Clause 6 — Planning** *(the heart of it)*
- 6.1.1 Actions to address risks and opportunities.
- 6.1.2 **An AI risk assessment process** — defined, repeatable, with criteria.
- 6.1.3 **AI risk treatment** — produces a **Risk Treatment Plan** and a **Statement of
  Applicability (SoA)**: for each of the 38 Annex A controls, is it applicable, is it implemented,
  and *why/why not*. The SoA is the single most scrutinized document in the audit.
- 6.1.4 **An AI system impact assessment process.** This is 42001's genuine addition over the generic
  ISO skeleton — assessing impacts on **individuals and society**, not just on the organization. ISO
  27001 has no equivalent.
- 6.2 Measurable AI objectives.
- 6.3 Planning of changes.

**Clause 7 — Support**
- 7.1 Resources · 7.2 **Competence** (with records) · 7.3 Awareness · 7.4 Communication ·
  7.5 Control of documented information.

**Clause 8 — Operation**
- 8.1 Operational planning and control (with records).
- 8.2 Perform AI risk assessment (recurring — not the one-off from Clause 6).
- 8.3 Implement AI risk treatment.
- 8.4 Perform AI system impact assessments, **and redo them after significant change.**

**Clause 9 — Performance evaluation**
- 9.1 Monitor, measure, analyse, evaluate.
- 9.2 **Internal audit** — programme *and* results.
- 9.3 **Management review** — with minutes.

**Clause 10 — Improvement**
- 10.1 Continual improvement.
- 10.2 Nonconformity and corrective action, documented.

## 3. Annex A — 38 controls across 9 objectives

Selectable via the SoA. Grouped as follows (control counts in brackets):

| Objective | Covers |
|---|---|
| **A.2 Policies related to AI** [3] | The AI policy itself, its alignment with other org policies, and its review. |
| **A.3 Internal organization** [2] | AI roles and responsibilities; **a route for reporting concerns**. |
| **A.4 Resources for AI systems** [5] | Documenting and managing the resources an AI system depends on: data, tooling, compute/system, and human resources. |
| **A.5 Assessing impacts of AI systems** [4] | The impact-assessment process, its documentation, impacts on individuals/groups, and societal impacts. |
| **A.6 AI system life cycle** [9] | The largest group. Responsible design/development objectives and processes; requirements specification; design/development documentation; **verification and validation**; deployment; **operation and monitoring**; technical documentation; **event logs**. |
| **A.7 Data for AI systems** [5] | Data for development, acquisition, **quality**, **provenance**, and preparation. |
| **A.8 Information for interested parties** [4] | System documentation and user information; external reporting; **incident communication**; information for interested parties. |
| **A.9 Use of AI systems** [3] | Responsible-use processes and objectives; **intended use** (and by implication, misuse). |
| **A.10 Third-party and customer relationships** [3] | Allocation of responsibilities; suppliers; customers. |

**The groups that will cost us real engineering work:** A.6 (lifecycle — V&V, monitoring, event logs)
and A.7 (data quality and provenance). Those are code and infrastructure, not policy. A.10 is the one
that governs the vendor↔client relationship itself, and A.8 is where the incident-communication duty
to the client lives.

**Annex B** gives implementation guidance per control (informative, no new requirements).
**Annex C** offers a menu of AI objectives (fairness, transparency, safety, accountability, privacy,
robustness, environmental impact) and a catalogue of risk sources — useful raw material for Clause
6.1.2 and 6.2. **Annex D** covers applying the AIMS across sectors and integrating it with ISO 27001 /
9001 / 31000.

| **Statement of Applicability** | 6.1.3 |
| AI system impact assessment process + reports | 6.1.4 / 8.4 |
| Measurable AI objectives | 6.2 |

## 5. Roles — and why we hold more than one

42001 assigns obligations by role, and the applicable Annex A controls differ accordingly:

| Role | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **AI producer** | Designs, builds, trains the AI. |
| **AI provider** | Delivers the AI product/platform to a customer. |
| **AI customer** | Procures and uses AI. |
| **AI partner** | Supplies components/data/services without full control of behaviour. |
| **AI subject** | The individual or group affected by the AI. |

**A platform vendor is simultaneously producer and provider** — it builds the agents *and* delivers
the platform. The client is the **AI customer**. The client's staff (POs, QA engineers) whose work
the agents shape are arguably **AI subjects**; downstream individuals (e.g. patients) are not when
no decision about them is being made — worth stating explicitly in scope, because it materially
reduces the impact-assessment burden.

Meanwhile **the cloud model service (e.g. AWS/Bedrock) is an AI partner**, and the model developer
behind it is upstream **AI producer** — which is what makes A.10 (suppliers) and GOVERN 6.x in AI
RMF non-optional for the vendor.

This dual producer/provider status is normal and expected — it parallels the EU AI Act's
provider/deployer distinction, where one entity can be both. But it means **the AIMS scope statement
(4.3) must name every role we hold, per AI system.** Get that wrong and the SoA is wrong, and the SoA
is what the auditor reads first.

## 6. Certification path — what it actually costs

Two-stage initial audit, then a **3-year certificate**:

1. **Stage 1** (1–2 days) — documentation and readiness review. Is the scope coherent? Is there a
   real risk methodology? Is the SoA credible?
2. **Stage 2** (3–9+ days, scope-dependent) — operational effectiveness. Did the loop actually run?
   Sampled Annex A controls, interviews with control owners, evidence from Clauses 8–10.
3. **Years 2 and 3** — annual surveillance audits, roughly a third of the effort.
4. **Year 4** — recertification, new cycle.

**The gating reality:** Stage 2 needs *evidence that the system has been operating*. You cannot write
the documents in a month and certify. You need internal audits, management reviews, and monitoring
records that have actually accumulated. Realistically **6–12 months of a running AIMS** before a
Stage 2 is passable.

Related standards: **ISO/IEC 42006** sets requirements for the certification bodies themselves (the
analogue of 27006 for 27001) — its maturity is what makes 42001 certification credible.
**ISO/IEC 42005** (2025) tells you how to conduct the impact assessment that 6.1.4 and A.5 demand.
**ISO/IEC 23894** is the risk-management guidance behind 6.1.2. **ISO/IEC 22989** is the terminology.

## 7. Honest assessment for a single-client engagement

**Do not chase ISO 42001 certification for one engagement.** It is 6–12 months and real money, and
a client rarely asks for it directly; what they ask (in effect) is "prove our data doesn't leave
and prove the AI doesn't make things up."

**But do build against its structure**, for two reasons:

1. **Almost everything 42001 requires, AI RMF also asks for** — and AI RMF is free and immediate. The
   overlap is high (see [03-crosswalk.md](03-crosswalk.md)). Doing AI RMF properly *is* 42001
   groundwork, so the vendor does not pay twice.
2. **If the strategic thesis is a reusable platform**, "an operating system for governed AI usage"
   is a much easier thing to sell to the *next* client if there is a certifiable management system
   behind it. That is a sales-strategy conversation, not a per-engagement one.

The cheap, high-value subset to do now, regardless: an **AI policy**, an **AI system impact
assessment** for each agent, a **risk register**, and **event logs + monitoring** (A.6). Those four
are demanded by AI RMF and HITRUST anyway.
